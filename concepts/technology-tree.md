# RUIN technology tree

The tree turns science-fiction motifs into independent infrastructure problems. It deliberately avoids reproducing fictional weapon specifications. Instead, it asks what a civilization must operate, manufacture, certify, repair, and govern before more speculative capabilities can exist.

## Four horizons

1. **Autonomous foothold** — closed-loop control, in-situ refining, fusion power, delay-tolerant communications, and layered impact protection.
2. **Industrial expansion** — partial machine replication, rare-material provenance, reconfigurable factories, beamed power, electric fleets, and salvage tugs.
3. **System-scale civilization** — stellar collectors, trusted navigation beacons, guided capture corridors, and distributed energy coordination.
4. **Speculative frontier** — adaptive protective fields, causality-aware instant relays, and metric transit gates.

The executable graph is in `src/technologyTree.ts`. It marks every node as **demonstrated**, **extrapolated**, or **speculative**, and will not unlock a technology until all prerequisite IDs are complete.

## Design principles extracted from the reference

- Energy generation is not enough; distribution, heat rejection, and safe shutdown dominate operations.
- Exotic materials become a provenance and supply-chain problem before they become a performance advantage.
- Fast travel requires destination beacons, clock agreement, exclusion corridors, and exactly-once arrival semantics.
- A fleet is a mobile infrastructure graph: power, cooling, communication, repair, rescue, and cargo certification.
- Compact or reconfigurable equipment creates verification problems whenever it changes form or ownership.
- Remote energy sharing and contactless capture are most valuable for rescue, construction, and recovery.

## Safety and originality boundary

Weapon construction, targeting, munition effects, human enhancement recipes, and destructive planetary systems are outside RUIN's implementation scope. Related motifs are transformed into shielding allocation, collision avoidance, medical autonomy, logistics, reliability, or fictional consistency problems.
